## Problem 2

For each of the following fields, either prove that the field is a splitting field over $$\mathbb Q$$, or prove that it is not.

* (a) $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{5})$$

Not a splitting field!  Splitting fields are finite and normal extensions.
It is not normal, since $$x^3-5$$ has a root in it but does not split in it.

* (b) $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{i})$$

Yes, it's a splitting field of $$x^4+1$$ because $$\sqrt{i} = \omega_4$$, so $$1,\omega_4,\omega_4^2,\omega_4^3\in\mathbb Q(\sqrt{i})$$.
Therefore

$$x^4+1=(x-1)(x-\omega_4)(x-\omega_4^2)(x-\omega_4^3)$$

splits over $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{i})$$.  Also $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{i}) = \mathbb Q(1,\omega_4,\omega_4^2,\omega_4^3)$$.
This is the definition of be the **definition** of a splitting field.

* (c) $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$$

Yes, it's a splitting field of $$(x^2-2)(x^2-3)$$ because $$\pm\sqrt{2}$$ and $$\pm\sqrt{3}$$ are in $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$$.
Therefore

$$(x^2-2)(x^2-3) = (x-\sqrt{2})(x+\sqrt{2})(x-\sqrt{3})(x+\sqrt{3}).$$

Therefore $$(x^2-2)(x^2-3)$$ splits over $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})$$.  Also $$\mathbb Q(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}) = \mathbb Q(\sqrt{2},-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3},-\sqrt{3})$$
This is the definition of be the **definition** of a splitting field.

* (d) $$\mathbb Q(\pi)$$

Not a splitting field!  Splitting fields are finite and normal extensions.
If it was a finite extension then it would be an algebraic extension, forcing every element to be algebraic.  
The element $$\pi$$ is transcendental over $$\mathbb Q$$, so this can't be the case.

## Problem 3

Give an example of each of the following

* (a) A field which is not separable

The field $$\mathbb F_3(t)$$ of rational functions with coefficients in $$\mathbb F_3$$.

* (b) A ring which is not a UFD

$$\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$$

* (c) An algebraic field extension of $$\mathbb{Q}$$ with infinite degree

The **algebraic closure** $$\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$$ of all complex numbers which are algebraic over $$\mathbb Q$$ is a field which has infinite degree.

* (d) A field extension whose Galois group is isomorphic to $$\mathbb Z_6$$

In general the Galois group of $$\mathbb Q(\omega_n)$$ is 

$$\text{Gal}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}(\omega_n)) = \{\sigma_k: 1\leq k \leq n,\ \gcd(n,k)=1\}.$$

Here $$\sigma_k: \mathbb Q(\omega_n)\rightarrow \mathbb Q(\omega_n)$$ is the unique field automorphism satisfying $$\sigma_k(\omega_n) = \omega_n^k$$.

Moreover, it is isomorphic to $$\mathbb Z_n^\times$$, the group of integers between $$1$$ and $$n$$ which are relatively prime to $$n$$ (with binary operation given by multiplication modulo $$n$$).

For $$n=7$$, we get the Galois group is $$\mathbb{Z}_7^\times\cong \mathbb Z_6$$.

## Problem 4

* (a) Write down the definition of a Euclidean domain

It is an integral domain $$R$$ with a Euclidean function $$\rho: \mathbb R-\{0\}\rightarrow \mathbb Z_+=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$$ with the property that for any $$a,b\in R$$ there exists $$q,r\in R$$ with 

$$a = qb + r\quad\text{and}\quad r=0\ \text{or}\ \rho(r) < \rho(b).$$

* (b) Write down the definition of a PID

It is an integral domain where every ideal is principal.

* (b) Write down the definition of a GCD domain

It is an integral domain where every pair of nonzero elements has a greatest common divisor.

* (c) Prove that any Euclidean domain is a PID

Suppose that $$R$$ is a Euclidean domain and that $$I\subseteq R$$ is an ideal.  Let

$$n = \min\{\rho(r): r\in I,\ r\neq 0\},$$

which exists by the Well-ordering Principle and choose $$b\in I$$ with $$\rho(b) = r$$.
Then since $$b\in I$$, we know $$(b)\subseteq I$$.
Conversely, for any $$a\in I$$ we can write $$a=bq + r$$ for some $$q,r\in R$$ with $$r=0$$ or $$\rho(r)<n=\rho(b)$$.
Since $$r=a-bq\in I$$, we can't have $$\rho(r)<n$$, so we must have $$r=0$$ and $$a=qb\in (b).$$  This proves that $$I\subseteq (b)$$.
Hence $$I=(b)$$, proving that $$I$$ is principal.

* (d) Prove that any PID is a GCD domain

Let $$R$$ be a PID and suppose that $$a,b\in R$$ are nonzero.  Consider the ideal $$I=(a,b)$$.  Since $$I$$ is a PID, we know that there exists $$c\in R$$ with $$I=(c)$$.  Since $$a\in I$$, this means that there exists $$p\in R$$ with $$a=pc$$.  Hence $$c$$ divides $$a$$.  Likewise, $$c$$ divides $$b$$.

If $$d$$ is another common divisor of both $$a$$ and $$b$$, then the same argument can be used to show $$a,b\in (d)$$.  Therefore $$I=(a,b)\subseteq (d)$$.
This means that $$(c)\subseteq (d)$$, so that $$c\in (d)$$.
Hence there exists $$r\in R$$ with $$c=rd$$, so that $$d$$ divides $$c$$.  This proves that $$c$$ satisfies the definition of a greatest common divisor of $$a$$ and $$b$$.  Since $$a,b\in R$$ were arbitrary nonzero elements, we conclude that $$R$$ is a GCD domain.

## Problem 5

Let $$m$$ and $$n$$ be positive integers.  

* (a) Write down the descent criterion for groups.

Let $$N$$ be a normal subgroup of a group $$G$$ and let $$\varphi: G\rightarrow H$$ be a group homomorphism.  If $$N\subseteq\ker(\varphi)$$, then $$\varphi$$ descends to the quotient to a group homomorphism $$\overline{\varphi}: G/N\rightarrow H$$.

* (b) Write down the descent criterion for rings.

Let $$I$$ be an ideal of a ring $$R$$ and let $$\varphi: R\rightarrow S$$ be a ring homomorphism.  If $$I\subseteq\ker(\varphi)$$, then $$\varphi$$ descends to the quotient to a group homomorphism $$\overline{\varphi}: R/I\rightarrow S$$.

* (c) Determine, with proof, for which values of $$m$$ and $$n$$ there is a nonzero group homomorphism from $$\mathbb Z_m$$ to $$\mathbb Z_n$$

We claim that a map exists if and only if $$\gcd(m,n) > 1$$.

Suppose that there was a nonzero group homomorphism $$\mathbb Z_m\rightarrow Z_n$$.
Then there would be a nonzero group homomorphism $$\mathbb Z/\langle m\rangle \rightarrow \mathbb Z/\langle n\rangle$$.
This would imply the existence of a nonzero group homomorphism $$\varphi: \mathbb Z \rightarrow \mathbb Z/\langle n\rangle$$ which satisfies the descent criterion for the normal subgroup $$N=\langle m\rangle$$ of $$\mathbb Z$$, ie. which satisfies $$\varphi(m) = 0+\langle n\rangle$$.
Any homomorphism out of $$\mathbb Z$$ is determined by where it sends $$1$$.  If $$\varphi(1) = k + \langle n\rangle$$, then the descent criterion requires $$\varphi(m) = km+\langle n\rangle = 0+\langle n\rangle$$.
This happens if and only if $$km\equiv 0\mod n$$, ie. $$n$$ divides $$km$$.  Since the map must be nonzero, $$k$$ cannot be divisible by $$n$$.  Therefore we must have $$\gcd(m,n) > 1$$.  This proves "only if".

When $$\gcd(m,n) > 1$$ then taking $$k=m/\gcd(m,n)$$ the map $$\varphi: \mathbb Z\rightarrow \mathbb Z/\langle n\rangle$$ defined by sending $$j$$ to $$jk+\langle n\rangle$$ is nonzero and descends to the quotient, so it does the job.  This proves "if".

* (d) Determine, with proof, for which values of $$m$$ and $$n$$ there is a nonzero ring homomorphism from $$\mathbb Z_m$$ to $$\mathbb Z_n$$

We claim that a map exists if and only if $$n$$ divides $$m$$.

Suppose that there was a nonzero ring homomorphism $$\mathbb Z_m\rightarrow Z_n$$.
Then there would be a nonzero ring homomorphism $$\mathbb Z/(m) \rightarrow \mathbb Z/(n)$$.
This would imply the existence of a nonzero group homomorphism $$\varphi: \mathbb Z \rightarrow \mathbb Z/(n)$$ which satisfies the descent criterion for the ideal $$I=(m)$$ of $$\mathbb Z$$, ie. which satisfies $$\varphi(m) = 0+(n)$$.
Any homomorphism out of $$\mathbb Z$$ is determined by where it sends $$1$$.  However, to be a *ring* homomorphism, it must send the identity to the identity.  Therefore $$\varphi(1) = 1 + (n)$$, and the descent criterion requires $$\varphi(m) = m+(n) = 0+(n)$$.
This happens if and only if $$m\equiv 0\mod n$$, ie. $$n$$ divides $$m$$.  This proves "only if".

When $$n$$ divides $$m$$, then the map $$\varphi: \mathbb Z\rightarrow \mathbb Z/(n)$$ defined by sending $$j$$ to $$j+\langle n\rangle$$ is nonzero and descends to the quotient, so it does the job.  This proves "if".

## Problem 6

Let $$F\subseteq K$$ be a normal field extension of degree $$n$$.

* (a) Write down the definition of a normal field extension.

A field extension $$K$$ of $$F$$ is normal if every irreducible polynomial in $$F[x]$$ with a root in $$K$$ must split over $$K$$.

* (b) Write down the definition of a splitting field.

A field extension $$K$$ of $$F$$ is a splitting field of a polynomial $$f(x)\in F[x]$$ if it satisfies the following two criteria

  - the polynomial splits over $$K$$:

$$f(x) = c(x-u_1)(x-u_2)\dots(x-u_n)$$ 

for some $$u_1,u_2,\dots, u_n\in K$$

  - the extension is generated by the roots

$$K = F(u_1,u_2,\dots, u_n)$$

* (c) Prove that $$K$$ must be the splitting field of some polynomial in $$F[x]$$.

Choose a basis $$u_1,\dots, u_n$$ of $$K$$ over $$F$$ and for each $$u_k$$ let $$p_k(x)\in F[x]$$ be the minimal polynomial of $$u_k$$.
Then since $$K$$ is normal and each $$p_k(x)$$ is irreducible it must split over $$K$$, ie.

$$p_k(x) = (x-u_{k1})(x-u_{k2})\dots (x-u_{kn_k}).$$

where here $$u_{k1} = u_k$$ for all $$k$$.

Then $$p(x) = p_1(x)p_2(x)\dots p_n(x)$$ splits over $$K$$ since

$$p(x) = \prod_{k=1}^n\prod_{j=1}^{n_k} (x-u_{kj}).$$

Moreover since each $$u_{kj}\in K$$ we have

$$K\supseteq \mathbb Q(u_{11},u_{12},\dots, u_{nn_k})\supseteq Q(u_{11},u_{21},\dots, u_{n1})=\mathbb Q(u_1,u_2,\dots, u_n)=K.$$

This proves that $$K$$ is the splitting field of $$p(x)$$.

## Problem 7

Let $$m$$ and $$n$$ be positive integers.

* (a) State the First Isomorphism Theorem for groups.

Let $$\varphi: G\rightarrow H$$ be a group homomorphism.  Then $$\ker(\varphi)$$ is a normal subgroup of $$G$$ and

$$G/\ker(\varphi)\cong \text{img}(\varphi).$$

* (b) Determine the image and the kernel of the group homomorphsm

$$f: \mathbb Z\rightarrow Z_{m}\times\mathbb Z_n,\quad f(x) = (x\mod m,x\mod n).$$

The kernel is

$$\begin{align}
\ker(\varphi)
&= \{x\in\mathbb Z: \varphi(x) = (0,0)\}\\
&= \{x\in\mathbb Z: x=0\mod m\ \text{and}\ x=0\mod n\}\\
&= \{x\in\mathbb Z:\ \text{$m$ and $n$ divide $x$}\}\\
&= \{x\in\mathbb Z:\ \text{$\text{lcm}(m,n)$ divides $x$}\}\\
&= (\text{lcm}(m,n))
\end{align}$$


The image is

$$\begin{align}
\text{img}(\varphi)
&= \{\varphi(x): x\in\mathbb{Z}\}\\
&= \{(x\mod m,x\mod n): x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
\end{align}$$

Suppose that $$(y,z)$$ is in the image.  Then $$0\leq y < m$$ and $$0\leq z < n$$ with $$y=x\mod m$$ and $$z=x\mod n$$.  Therefore $$x=y+jm$$ and $$x=z+kn$$ so that $$y-z = kn-jm$$.  This means that $$y-z$$ is divisible by $$\gcd(m,n)$$.  Conversely, if $$0\leq y < m$$ and $$0\leq z < n$$ are two integers with the property that $$\gcd(m,n)$$ divides $$y-z$$, then by Bezout's identity allows us to find $$a$$ and $$b$$ with $$an+bm=y-z$$.  Then if we let $$x=z+an$$, we have $$x=y-bm$$ so that $$\varphi(x)=(x,y)$$.  Thus

$$\text{img}(\varphi) = \{(y,z):\ 0\leq y < m,\ 0\leq z< n,\ \text{$\gcd(m,n)$ divides y-z}\}$$

* (c) If $$\gcd(m,n)=1$$, use part (b) to prove that $$\mathbb Z_{mn}$$ is isomorphic to $$\mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_n$$.

In the case that $$\gcd(m,n) =1$$, we also have $$\text{lcm}(m,n) = mn$$.
Therefore $$\varphi$$ is surjective with kernel $$(mn)$$ the ideal generated by $$mn$$.  By application of the First Isomorphism Theorem, we have 

$$\mathbb Z_{mn} \cong \mathbb Z/(mn)\cong \mathbb Z_m\times \mathbb Z_n$$.
